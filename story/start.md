The city never really slept. It just dimmed its lights and whispered in code.

At 3:17 a.m., when the skyline flickered with corporate logos and silent drones traced invisible grids overhead, Hex Voss was awake.

Hex didn’t believe in alarms. The network was the alarm.

A pulse rippled through the fiber lines beneath the streets — a subtle tremor in encrypted traffic, a stutter in a surveillance loop, a whisper buried inside a government subnet that wasn’t supposed to exist. Most people would call it static.

Hex called it **opportunity**.

Three monitors painted the room in cold blues and surgical greens. The apartment was small, anonymous — concrete walls, blackout curtains, cables coiled like sleeping serpents across the floor. On the desk: a custom rig with no manufacturer branding, liquid-cooled and overclocked past anything legal. Every fan curve tuned. Every LED stripped. No glow. No identity.

*Identity was a liability.*

Online, Hex Voss was a ghost threaded through the architecture of the city — slipping between firewalls, poisoning data lakes, rerouting secrets into cold storage vaults only they controlled. Corporations called Hex a terrorist. Governments used quieter words: anomaly, persistent threat, asset pending acquisition.

Hex preferred a simpler title.

**Intrusion artist.**

A new signal blinked on the far-left monitor. Encrypted. Unregistered. Origin masked but sloppy — deliberate sloppiness. A lure.

On-screen, a single line appeared:

**WE KNOW WHAT YOU TOOK.**

Below it, a countdown began.

```
02:59
02:58
02:57
```

Somewhere in the city, a server farm shifted power loads. A satellite adjusted orbit by half a degree. A camera that wasn’t supposed to exist pivoted toward Hex’s building.

The game wasn’t theoretical anymore.

Hex leaned back in the chair, eyes reflecting lines of cascading code. A faint smile cut through the monitor glow.

“Finally,” they murmured.

The cursor blinked.

The countdown continued.

And the city held its breath.
