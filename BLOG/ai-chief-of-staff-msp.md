<h1 align="center">How We’re Using an AI Chief of Staff Inside a Real MSP</h1>

At NanoTech Computing (a DBA of NTCHELP, INC.), a lot of the week is the same kinds of work over and over: triage an inbox, draft a clear client reply, build a simple intake form, dig through old mail for a vendor, research a tool, or chase a due date. None of that replaces an engineer on a real ticket. It is the glue work that steals hours.

We run that glue through Grok Bot — not as one magic chatbot, but as a small roster with a chief of staff that routes work and specialists that own a lane. One bot owns research briefs. Another owns brand or store ops when we need them. Another watches calendar-style due dates and nudges before something is due. Another is a final QA gate so drafts and PDFs get a hard look before anything ships. The point is ownership: one clear owner per job, not a pile of half-finished threads.

## Where the time actually goes back

On repeatable tasks, the pattern is edit-and-approve instead of blank-page writing. A client email that used to take twenty to forty minutes of drafting often becomes a short review: tone, facts, signature left alone, then send. Supplier diligence that used to burn an afternoon becomes a structured brief pulled from mail history and public sources. Fillable intake forms and “what do we need before we touch the server” checklists compress the back-and-forth that used to live in five reply chains. Weekly status across the bot roster replaces “what is everyone working on?” with one short overview of done, blocked, and stuck.

We are careful not to invent stopwatch theater. The honest claim is hours back per week on work we already know how to do — not that every ticket is autonomous. Humans still decide, still touch production systems, and still own the relationship.

## Cost: the $30 plan is a starting point, not our ceiling

People often hear about a low monthly plan in the ballpark of thirty dollars. For light personal use, that can be enough. A busy MSP stack — several specialist bots, Gmail and calendar connectors, store or admin APIs, agent computers that open browsers and build files — burns more usage. We treat the cheap tier as a floor to test ideas, not as a promise that our real workload fits there. Cost-effectiveness is labor hours avoided versus total subscription and usage. If the bill climbs, the question is still: did we buy back more billable or salvageable time than we spent? Often yes on drafts, research, and admin; less so if you ask the system to do work that still needs a tech on-site or in the hypervisor.

## Plugging in the stack with APIs and connectors

The useful part is not chat. It is wiring. Connect mail once and the bot can search threads, draft replies, and leave send under human control. Connect drive or docs and intake PDFs become a deliverable instead of a weekend project. Connect calendar for availability and reminders. Connect store or tax-adjacent tools where the API allows read of what you owe and when. Remote tools fit the same idea: least privilege keys, named purpose, and no silent sends to customers. The habit we keep: approve before anything leaves under the company name.
